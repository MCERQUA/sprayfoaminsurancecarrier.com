Off-ratio spray foam is one of the most significant — and most misunderstood — risks in the specialty contractor insurance market. For underwriters, it represents a complex challenge: the claim often surfaces long after the job is complete, the cause can be difficult to establish, and the remediation costs can be substantial.

For contractors, the stakes are equally high. Most standard GL policies exclude off-ratio claims. Without specific off-ratio coverage, a single improper-mix job can generate an uninsured loss that threatens the business.

Here's how insurance carriers actually underwrite off-ratio spray foam risk — and what it means for your coverage program.

## What Is Off-Ratio Spray Foam?

Spray foam is a two-component system. Component A (isocyanate) and Component B (polyol blend) are combined through a proportioner at a precise ratio — typically 1:1 by volume, though this varies by product. When the ratio falls outside the manufacturer's specified tolerance, the foam may:

- Fail to fully cure, leaving a tacky or soft surface
- Lose adhesion to the substrate
- Emit chemical odors or gases as unreacted chemicals off-gas
- Create a surface that degrades more rapidly than properly mixed foam
- In extreme cases, cause structural compromise in the application substrate

The resulting claims — property damage, habitability complaints, remediation costs — are the "off-ratio claim" that insurers must decide whether to cover or exclude.

## Why Standard GL Excludes Off-Ratio Claims

Standard commercial general liability policies contain what's commonly called the "your work" or "faulty workmanship" exclusion. This exclusion removes coverage for property damage to the work itself caused by defective workmanship.

Off-ratio foam is treated as defective workmanship by most standard GL carriers. The foam failed to perform as expected because of how it was applied — that's a workmanship issue, and the exclusion applies.

The irony is that the downstream consequences of off-ratio foam — an adjacent surface damaged by off-gassing, a building occupant who reports respiratory effects, remediation work that damages other building components — may trigger third-party property damage or bodily injury claims. Whether GL responds to these third-party consequences (while excluding the foam itself) depends on the specific policy language.

E&S spray foam GL programs either modify the workmanship exclusion or add off-ratio coverage explicitly, so the entire loss — the foam, the remediation, and the third-party consequences — is covered under one policy.

## How Underwriters Assess Off-Ratio Risk

When an E&S carrier underwriters a spray foam GL application, off-ratio is a primary consideration. Here's what underwriters look at:

**Equipment calibration practices.** Underwriters want to know how frequently the proportioner is calibrated, what the calibration process looks like, and whether calibration records are maintained. A contractor who calibrates equipment daily and documents it is a materially different risk than one who calibrates annually.

**Manufacturer training and certification.** Manufacturer-certified applicators have demonstrated knowledge of the equipment and product specifications. Underwriters view certification documentation favorably — it indicates the contractor understands the ratio requirements and has been trained to maintain them.

**Job documentation.** Application logs that record substrate temperature, ambient conditions, equipment settings, and mix ratio compliance serve two purposes: they support favorable underwriting, and they're essential documentation if an off-ratio claim is later disputed. A contractor with good job records has a significantly stronger claims position.

**Loss history.** Prior off-ratio claims or complaints are underwritten carefully. A single isolated incident with documented corrective action is handled differently than a pattern of off-ratio losses. Underwriters look at the claim circumstances, the contractor's response, and whether underlying equipment or process issues were corrected.

**System and product mix.** Not all spray foam systems present equal off-ratio risk. Two-component systems with integrated ratio monitoring, automatic shutoff when ratio deviates, and high-precision metering present lower risk than older systems without these controls. Underwriters account for the equipment in the pricing and terms.

**Job type mix.** Residential open-cell insulation and commercial closed-cell spray foam present different risk profiles. Spray foam roofing has a different completed-operations exposure than building cavity fill. Underwriters look at the revenue breakdown by job type to price the specific risk.

## What Off-Ratio Coverage Actually Covers

Off-ratio coverage — whether as a GL endorsement or a standalone policy — typically covers:

**Property damage to the work.** The cost to remove and replace defective off-ratio foam is usually covered under off-ratio-specific forms. This is the expense the standard GL "your work" exclusion would otherwise eliminate.

**Property damage to adjacent work.** If the off-ratio foam damages other building components — walls, roofing systems, mechanical systems — this is covered as third-party property damage.

**Remediation costs.** Removing spray foam that has failed to adhere or is off-gassing is expensive. Scaffolding, containment, chemical handling disposal, and labor are all part of a remediation claim.

**Third-party bodily injury.** If occupants or workers experience health effects from off-gassing foam — respiratory irritation, sensitization reactions — these bodily injury claims are covered.

**Defense costs.** A contractor named in a lawsuit over off-ratio foam has defense costs regardless of the outcome. Off-ratio coverage includes defense, and for complex completed-operations claims, defense can be a significant expense.

## The Subjectivities Carriers Require

Before binding off-ratio coverage, carriers typically require what insurers call "subjectivities" — conditions the contractor must satisfy:

- **Manufacturer certification documentation** for all applicators
- **Current equipment calibration records** (typically within the past 6–12 months)
- **Written application quality-control protocol** (what the contractor does to verify ratio compliance on each job)
- **Loss control questionnaire** detailing equipment, job types, crew training, and safety procedures
- **5-year loss history** from prior carriers

Meeting these subjectivities not only gets the policy bound — it typically produces better pricing. Underwriters reward documented processes because they reduce the likelihood of an off-ratio claim.

## Claims: What Happens When an Off-Ratio Loss Occurs

When an off-ratio claim is filed, the carrier will investigate:

- Was the foam installed at the proper ratio? (Equipment calibration records and application logs are examined)
- Was the failure in the foam itself, or did external factors contribute? (Substrate preparation, ambient conditions, product storage)
- What are the actual damages? (Remediation scope, third-party claims, bodily injury reports)

Contractors with good documentation are in a significantly stronger position. An insurer who can establish that the foam was applied within specification has grounds to dispute a claim. A contractor without documentation has no basis to rebut an allegation of off-ratio application.
